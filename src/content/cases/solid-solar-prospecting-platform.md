<b>My role:</b> Product lead. I shaped the proposition, made scope and product decisions, wrote requirements and ran delivery with our designer and two developers. The founder owned the underlying data work and most customer outreach.

## Making the data useful

Solar installers can spend a huge amount of time jumping between satellite imagery, company registers and contact databases. We wanted to bring that work into one place.

I structured SOLID around a simple journey:

- <b>Scout.</b> Explore properties on a map, search by location or company, and filter opportunities.
- <b>Qualify.</b> Check roof area, solar potential, existing panel coverage and the company behind the building.
- <b>Save.</b> Build a list of Prospects to revisit and manage.
- <b>Get the lead.</b> Access richer company and contact details to prepare outreach.

## The decisions that mattered

<b>Map or list?</b> We considered both. I chose a map for scouting territories and individual buildings, then a list for managing saved prospects. It felt like a natural fit for how installers assess an area.

<b>What happens when the data gets messy?</b> One company could have several buildings. Property and business records came from different sources, and some fields were missing. I worked through search, selection and information hierarchy with UX and engineering so users could understand what they were looking at.

<b>How much should we build?</b> With one frontend and one backend developer, I kept the backlog focused on the complete prospecting journey. I set up our GitHub Project, wrote the issues and worked through dependencies with the team. Billing and subscriptions could wait until we had a product worth demonstrating.

## And then we met the market

We got the core application into production, and the founder started demonstrating it to solar installation companies in Portugal.

The conversations helped sharpen the product. We reviewed lead contacts through RocketReach, improved how prospects and rooftop coverage were handled, and postponed a larger data-integration commitment until we could establish its value. Our data checks were encouraging, with accuracy around the 90% level we had targeted.

But winning customers was harder than building the software. Portugal was a relatively small, established market, with competitors already serving installers. Development stopped before we could prove paid acquisition, subscriptions or retention. Spain was a possible next market, but we didn't reach that stage.

## What I'd do differently

I'm proud of what our small team delivered. We took a broad idea, worked through some genuinely complicated data and built a product the founder could put in front of customers.

If I were doing it again, I'd test the willingness to switch from existing tools and the customer acquisition channel much earlier. We built a useful product. The harder question was how we'd turn it into a business.
